## 2.1 Thematic Review of Literature and Technical Evidence

### 2.1.1 Outcome-Based Education and continuous quality improvement

Outcome-Based Education (OBE) places emphasis on what learners are expected to know, do, or demonstrate at the end of an educational process and on the evidence used to determine whether those outcomes have been attained. In the Philippine higher education context, the Commission on Higher Education (CHED) has long incorporated outcomes-based and typology-based quality assurance into its quality framework. Its 2022 policy on quality and quality assurance further emphasizes a culture of quality supported by external review and verifiable evidence (Commission on Higher Education \[CHED\], 2022).

The importance of evidence does not end with determining whether an outcome was achieved. Quality assurance processes also depend on how findings are interpreted and used for improvement. Current accreditation criteria similarly require documented processes for assessing student outcomes and using the results as input to continuous improvement (ABET, 2026). Samuel and Farrer (2025) describe the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle as a practical way of connecting evidence collection, analysis, improvement actions, and subsequent review in higher education.

These ideas are relevant to CLOIE because the system is intended to organize evidence that can support an institution's existing evaluation and improvement processes. System CLOIE does not determine whether an academic program is compliant or whether a curriculum should be changed. Those decisions remain with authorized academic personnel. Its role is to make relevant outcome and evaluation evidence easier to collect, organize, analyze, and review.

### 2.1.2 Learning outcomes assessment and curriculum alignment

Learning outcomes assessment requires institutions to translate broad educational goals into observable or otherwise assessable evidence. Goss (2022) describes student learning outcomes assessment as a complex process that requires institutions to connect outcomes with evidence and use assessment findings in educational decision-making. Alyasin et al. (2023) similarly describe a systematic assessment cycle in which learning outcomes are connected to program objectives and curriculum structures through assessment plans, curriculum mapping, rubrics, and direct and indirect measures.

The connection between outcomes and curriculum is also visible in established assessment systems. Penn State's assessment guidance, for example, describes curriculum maps that connect program learning outcomes to courses and identify the level at which an outcome is expected to be encountered, practiced, or mastered. Its Nuventive-based assessment process also links outcomes, assessment methods, findings, and improvement actions.

Research has also shown that ordinary learning management systems do not necessarily provide all the functions needed for systematic intended learning outcome assessment. El Marsafawy et al. (2022) identified a gap between accreditation requirements and commonly used LMS functionality and developed a framework for measuring course- and program-level learning outcomes.

For CLOIE, the important point is that learning outcomes need to remain connected to their academic context. A CILO is associated with a course, while program- and institutional-level outcomes provide broader reference points for interpretation. This supports the inclusion of outcome definitions, mapping relationships, evaluation evidence, and contextual information in System CLOIE rather than treating survey responses as isolated records.

### 2.1.3 Stakeholder-based academic evaluation

Academic quality cannot be examined from a single perspective. Students can provide information about their learning experiences and course delivery. Graduates can provide evidence about the relevance of their education after completing a program. Industry or workplace stakeholders can provide an external view of graduate competencies and preparedness.

Recent research illustrates the value of combining these perspectives. Sarsale et al. (2024), using a Philippine graduate tracer study, examined employment profiles, perceived program contributions to professional and personal growth, and dimensions of curricular relevance associated with employment success. Their findings show how graduate feedback can provide evidence about aspects of a program that may not be visible through course-level evaluation alone.

Employer feedback provides another perspective. Recent Philippine studies have used employer assessments to examine graduate competencies, curriculum relevance, and areas for improvement. For example, Castro et al. (2025) found that employer feedback could identify skills that institutions may need to strengthen as labor-market expectations change. Similar work on curriculum development has emphasized the value of incorporating industry and other stakeholder perspectives when reviewing academic programs (Nampala et al., 2024).

This supports CLOIE's use of multiple evaluation instruments rather than treating student evaluation as the sole source of academic feedback. In the project context, the relevant institutional instruments include the Post-Term CILO Evaluation Tool, Graduating Student Exit Survey, Alumni Evaluation Tool, and Industry Partner Internship Evaluation Tool. These are institution-specific evaluation instruments used as part of the CLOIE requirements, not instruments adopted from the literature.

### 2.1.4 Quality assurance, accreditation, and evidence management

Quality assurance requires more than the existence of evaluation forms. Institutions must be able to organize evidence, interpret findings, document actions, and retrieve information when it is needed for internal review or external assessment. CHED's current policy framework emphasizes quality assurance supported by evidence, while international accreditation criteria similarly emphasize documented assessment and continuous improvement processes (CHED, 2022; ABET, 2026).

The University of the Philippines provides a useful institutional example. Its QA Portal brings together internal quality assurance, external quality assurance, evidence submission, assessment and accreditation schedules, final reports, and post-assessment plans. The portal's current structure shows how quality assurance evidence can be managed as a continuing institutional process rather than as isolated accreditation documents.

This has direct implications for CLOIE. Evaluation results are more useful when they retain enough context to identify the relevant program, course, academic period, instrument, respondent group, and outcome. The system therefore needs to preserve relationships among academic structures, evaluation deployments, responses, outcomes, and reports. This makes the resulting information more suitable for later review and comparison.

### 2.1.5 Learning analytics and outcome-attainment reporting

Learning analytics can turn collected educational data into information that users can interpret and act upon. The value of analytics, however, depends on how the information is presented and what decisions it supports. Chen et al. (2023) found that students with stronger learning performance used learning analytics dashboards differently from lower-performing students, particularly in monitoring and reflection activities. Ramaswami et al. (2023) likewise found that learning analytics dashboards can provide useful descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive information, although dashboard design and interpretation remain important considerations.

For an academic management system such as CLOIE, the relevant application is somewhat different from dashboards designed primarily for individual learners. System CLOIE uses aggregated evaluation information to help authorized academic users examine patterns in responses, outcome attainment, and stakeholder feedback. The emphasis is therefore on program and course monitoring rather than on predicting individual student performance.

This distinction matters because analytics should not be treated as an automatic decision-maker. A dashboard can identify patterns or summarize results, but academic personnel still need to interpret the evidence within the institutional and curricular context. CLOIE therefore treats quantitative results as evidence for review rather than as automatic accreditation or curriculum decisions.

### 2.1.6 Qualitative feedback analysis

Quantitative ratings provide useful summaries, but they do not always explain why respondents selected a particular rating or what specific improvement they are suggesting. Open-ended responses can provide additional context, especially when respondents identify issues that were not anticipated by fixed-response items.

Research on educational evaluation increasingly combines quantitative and qualitative evidence to obtain a fuller picture of program performance. Recent employer and stakeholder studies, for example, have combined descriptive statistics with thematic analysis of open-ended feedback to identify specific strengths and areas for improvement.

This supports CLOIE's inclusion of qualitative feedback alongside numerical evaluation results. The system can preserve respondent comments and provide a qualitative summary such as word-cloud visualization where supported by the implementation. Such summaries are intended to help authorized users identify recurring terms or themes. They do not replace review of the underlying comments or constitute an automatic determination of program quality

### 2.1.7 Privacy, confidentiality, and security in academic evaluation systems

Academic evaluation systems may process personal information, account information, academic associations, and respondent feedback. The Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 requires personal information processing to follow the principles of transparency, legitimate purpose, and proportionality. It also requires appropriate safeguards for confidentiality, integrity, and availability (National Privacy Commission \[NPC\], 2012).

These principles are particularly relevant when evaluation responses are associated with identifiable accounts or eligibility records. A system may restrict who can see raw responses while still maintaining internal identifiers needed for eligibility, response integrity, or duplicate-response prevention. Consequently, describing all evaluation responses as "anonymous" would be inaccurate if the system retains an internal relationship between the respondent and the submitted response.

For CLOIE, the literature and regulatory context support role-based access, scope restrictions, controlled access to evaluation information, validation of submitted data, and careful handling of respondent information. They also support limiting access to raw qualitative comments where those comments could reveal information about a respondent.

### 2.1.8 Usability, accessibility, and adoption of academic information systems

A system intended for use by administrators, faculty, students, alumni, and industry partners must be understandable across different roles and devices. Usability is therefore a practical requirement rather than an optional interface concern. ISO/IEC 25010:2023 provides a current software product quality model that can be used as a reference when specifying and evaluating software quality characteristics (International Organization for Standardization \[ISO\], 2023).

Accessibility is also relevant because web-based academic systems may be used by people with different physical, sensory, and cognitive needs. WCAG 2.2 provides testable accessibility criteria covering areas such as keyboard operation, focus, target size, input assistance, and accessible authentication (World Wide Web Consortium \[W3C\], 2024).

For CLOIE, these principles support responsive interfaces, understandable navigation, clear validation messages, visible system states, and accessible interaction patterns. They also reinforce the need to evaluate usability with actual users rather than assuming that technically correct functionality is automatically easy to use.


## 2.2 Review of Related Systems / Existing Solutions

Several existing systems support learning outcomes assessment, quality assurance, academic evaluation, and reporting. This section reviews Nuventive/Penn State, Oklahoma State University's assessment platform, and the University of the Philippines Quality Assurance Portal based on their relevant functions, limitations, and applicability to System CLOIE and the context of Assumption College of Davao.

### 2.2.1 Nuventive and Penn State's assessment environment

Nuventive is an assessment management platform used by educational institutions to manage learning outcomes assessment, curriculum mapping, planning, and reporting. Penn State uses Nuventive as part of its learning outcomes assessment process, where academic units can document outcomes, map them to curriculum, plan assessments, record findings, and identify improvement actions. Penn State's curriculum mapping process also connects program learning outcomes with courses and their expected levels of learning.

The system is relevant to CLOIE because it demonstrates how learning outcomes, curriculum structures, assessment activities, and findings can be managed within a centralized system. Its assessment-management approach is similar to CLOIE's need to maintain relationships among courses, learning outcomes, evaluation activities, and results.

However, Nuventive operates as a broader institutional assessment platform intended for large academic environments. System CLOIE is being developed around the specific academic structure and evaluation workflows of ACD. CLOIE therefore focuses on the particular relationship between CILOs, programs, courses, academic periods, and the institution's evaluation instruments.

### 2.2.2 Oklahoma State University assessment platform using Nuventive and Azure

Oklahoma State University implemented an assessment environment using the Nuventive Improvement Platform together with Microsoft Azure and Power BI. The platform supports centralized assessment activities, dashboards, reports, and visualization of academic assessment information. Its implementation demonstrates how assessment data can be consolidated and presented to academic users for review and improvement.

The system is relevant to CLOIE because of its use of centralized assessment information and analytics. Dashboards and reports can reduce the need to manually consolidate assessment results and can help academic personnel identify patterns in collected evidence.

The main difference is the scale and technical environment of the implementation. Oklahoma State University's platform uses enterprise cloud services and business-intelligence tools within a large university environment. CLOIE has a narrower institutional scope and is designed around ACD's defined evaluation workflows and stakeholder groups. The comparison therefore provides a reference for assessment reporting and analytics without implying that CLOIE must reproduce an enterprise-scale implementation.

### 2.2.3 University of the Philippines Quality Assurance Portal

The University of the Philippines Quality Assurance Portal is a web-based platform that supports institutional quality assurance and accreditation-related activities. Its functions include internal and external quality assurance processes, submission of assessment documents, accreditation schedules, final reports, and post-assessment activities.

The portal is relevant to CLOIE because it demonstrates how quality-assurance information and supporting evidence can be managed through a centralized institutional system. Its Philippine higher-education context also makes it a useful reference when considering how digital systems can support institutional assessment and quality assurance.

Unlike CLOIE, the UP QA Portal is primarily organized around university-wide quality assurance and accreditation processes. System CLOIE focuses more specifically on learning-outcome and instructional evaluation, including course-level CILO evaluation and stakeholder evaluation. CLOIE's intended users, academic scopes, evaluation deployments, and outcome-related reporting are therefore defined around the requirements of ACD.

## 2.3 Synthesis, Gap and Project Contribution

The literature reviewed in this chapter shows that effective academic evaluation depends on structured learning outcomes, appropriate assessment evidence, stakeholder feedback, and processes for using results for improvement. The reviewed systems also show that these activities can be supported through centralized assessment, analytics, reporting, and quality-assurance platforms.

Existing solutions already provide many of the capabilities relevant to CLOIE. Nuventive and Penn State demonstrate structured learning-outcome assessment and curriculum mapping. Oklahoma State University's implementation demonstrates centralized assessment analytics and reporting. The University of the Philippines Quality Assurance Portal demonstrates the use of a centralized platform for quality-assurance and accreditation-related evidence.

However, these systems operate within different institutional settings and purposes. Their documented functions do not directly represent the specific evaluation workflow defined for Assumption College of Davao, particularly the combination of course-level CILO evaluation, program-level outcome relationships, and stakeholder evaluations involving graduating students, alumni, and industry partners.

The reviewed evidence supports the need for CLOIE requirements related to learning-outcome mapping, evaluation deployment, response collection, quantitative and qualitative analysis, reporting, and controlled access to evaluation information. These requirements translate the general practices identified in the literature into a system designed around ACD's academic structure and evaluation processes.

Project CLOIE therefore contributes an ACD-specific technological solution for organizing learning-outcome and instructional evaluation evidence. It is not intended to replace existing learning management, student information, or accreditation systems. Instead, System CLOIE provides a centralized environment for collecting, managing, analyzing, and reporting evaluation evidence that can support academic review and continuous quality improvement.

This synthesis provides the basis for the requirements, architecture, data design, security controls, and evaluation workflows presented in the succeeding chapters.
